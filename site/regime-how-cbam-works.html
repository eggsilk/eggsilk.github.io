CBAM regime · Understanding the regime

How CBAM works, end to end

In its definitive phase, CBAM operates as an annual corporate carbon accounting mandate rather than a customs duty paid at the border. Liability accrues continuously and is settled each year with certificates priced against the EU carbon market. The decisive variable is whether the importer can obtain verified, installation-level emissions data from non-EU manufacturers.

In brief

  • The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism operates as an annual corporate carbon accounting mandate rather than a customs duty collected at the border.
  • The primary operational hurdle is extracting verified, installation-level emissions data from non-EU manufacturers to avoid marked-up default values.
  • Financial liability accrues continuously and is settled annually using electronic certificates priced against volatile European carbon markets.

The definitive period

Since the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered its definitive period on 1 January 2026, the regulatory framework has shifted from a reporting exercise to a strict financial liability. The mechanism applies to specific imports (currently cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen), but the actual compliance process operates far upstream and downstream of the physical border crossing.

A common misconception frames CBAM as a tariff applied by a customs agent when a ship docks in Rotterdam or Hamburg. In practice, the customs process is merely a checkpoint. Customs authorities automatically transmit import data to the central CBAM Registry and validate whether the importing entity holds valid "Authorised CBAM Declarant" status. The physical clearance of goods does not trigger a point-of-sale carbon tax.

Instead, the legal and financial obligation sits entirely with the Authorised Declarant, typically the EU-based importer. This entity must perform an annual reconciliation. The declarant aggregates the total volume of covered goods imported over the previous calendar year, calculates the specific embedded carbon emissions of those goods, and surrenders a corresponding number of CBAM certificates by 30 September of the following year. The first surrender, covering 2026 imports, is due on 30 September 2027. Compliance is therefore an annual accounting process rather than a per-shipment charge.

The supply chain data mandate

The main operational difficulty in the definitive period lies less in purchasing certificates than in quantifying the underlying emissions. The calculation requires granular data: the exact tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent (CO₂e) emitted during the production of a specific batch of imported material at a specific non-EU manufacturing installation.

During the transitional period that ended in December 2025, the European Commission permitted EU importers to use self-reported estimates at no additional cost. In the definitive period, actual, primary emissions data is the intended route; where verified data is missing, marked-up default values apply instead, with no cap on their use.

If an EU importer cannot extract this primary data from their non-EU supplier, they fall back on EU-provided default values, which remain available without limit. The deterrent is the mark-up. Defaults apply a premium on top of country-average emission intensities (for aluminium, cement and iron and steel, 10% for 2026 imports rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers), set to incentivise the use of actual data. Relying on default values increases financial liability. For example, a non-EU steel mill producing highly efficient steel at 1.5 tCO₂e per tonne might be assigned a default value of 2.1 tCO₂e per tonne. At an estimated carbon price of €75 per tonne, the EU importer faces an unnecessary €45 premium per tonne of imported steel simply due to an upstream data failure.

To report actual data, the emissions must be audited by an independent accredited verifier. These verifiers perform mandatory physical site visits at the non-EU manufacturing installations. Because verifier registration in the CBAM Registry is scheduled to open in September 2026, and the pool of qualified auditors is limited, a verification capacity gap is currently forcing many importers to secure audit contracts months in advance. If an importer cannot secure a verifier, they have no alternative but to declare the marked-up default values.

Managing the financial instrument

CBAM certificates are electronic financial instruments held in a digital registry account. They are not issued by non-EU suppliers, nor do they travel with bills of lading. The EU importer purchases them directly from a central European platform.

The price of a CBAM certificate is directly linked to the live EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). For 2026 imports, the certificate price is set by the quarterly average auction price of EU ETS allowances, moving to a weekly average from 2027. This introduces market volatility to the supply chain. A mid-sized EU entity importing 10,000 tonnes of steel annually faces €150,000 to €300,000 in annual certificate costs at current carbon prices.

Because certificates for 2026 imports will become available for purchase from 1 February 2027, importers are currently accruing financial exposure without yet buying the instrument. Declarants treat this exposure similarly to currency risk. Rather than executing a single, highly exposed bulk purchase immediately before the 30 September surrender deadline, treasury departments are modelling costs based on ETS forecasts and planning phased purchases to average out price volatility.

The final certificate requirement can be reduced if a carbon price has already been paid in the country of origin. If a non-EU manufacturer paid a local carbon tax or purchased local compliance carbon credits for the specific emissions embedded in the exported goods, the EU importer can claim a deduction. This requires documented, verifiable proof of payment from the origin jurisdiction.

Enforcement and the cost escalator

Regulatory oversight is managed by national competent authorities in each EU Member State, and strict financial penalties are now active.

The penalty for failing to surrender the correct number of certificates by the annual deadline is €100 per tonne of CO₂e short. This fine is adjusted annually for inflation and operates as a strict penalty, not a buyout. The declarant must pay the €100 fine and still purchase and surrender the missing certificates at market rate.

Entities attempting to bypass the system face steeper consequences. Importing CBAM-covered goods without the required Authorised Declarant status triggers an immediate penalty of €300 to €500 per tonne of CO₂e, applied to the total embedded emissions of the non-compliant imports. Applications filed by 31 March 2026 allowed continued importing while approval was pending; applications remain possible after that date, but importing requires granted status. Customs systems flag and block goods linked to unauthorised entities.

The baseline cost of compliance will rise over the next decade. The European Union is simultaneously phasing out the free ETS allowances previously granted to domestic European manufacturers. As these free allowances drop to zero by 2034, the CBAM adjustment factor applied to imports rises proportionally. A default cost that is small per tonne in 2026 grows with the phase-in schedule and changes the economics of sourcing heavy industrial goods from outside the European Union.

Conclusion

CBAM forces EU importers to internalise the carbon accounting of their global supply chains. A company's financial exposure is determined by its ability to extract verified, installation-level data from non-EU manufacturers and its treasury's ability to manage variable European carbon prices. Importers relying on fragmented data collection fall back on marked-up default values, which raises the cost of their international supply lines.
