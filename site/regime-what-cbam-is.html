What CBAM is and why it exists

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism prices the carbon embedded in imported goods at the level an EU producer already pays. Since January 2026 it has been financially binding. What it means for a business turns on two questions: its role in the supply chain, and the code of the good it trades.

In brief

  • CBAM is an environmental equaliser that ties import costs to the EU ETS carbon price rather than to the monetary value of the goods.
  • Since January 2026, EU importers bear direct financial liability, while non-EU producers face commercial risk without verifiable emissions data.
  • The main lever for reducing the cost is demonstrating an equivalent carbon price already paid in the country of manufacture.

Scope and responsibility

Since 1 January 2026, the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism has operated in its definitive, financially binding phase. The transitional reporting period, which ran from October 2023 to December 2025, is over. What CBAM means for a business is determined by two questions: the entity's role in the supply chain, and the exact Harmonized System (HS) code of the traded product.

The product scope is narrow. CBAM currently applies only to cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen, and electricity. An importer is exempt only where its total mass of covered goods stays below a 50-tonne annual threshold, a de-minimis that does not apply to electricity or hydrogen. Businesses importing goods outside these categories carry no CBAM obligations at present.

For those in scope, the legal and financial burden rests entirely with the EU importer of record, or their appointed indirect customs representative. The importer must open a CBAM account with national authorities, calculate the embedded emissions of their imports, and surrender corresponding CBAM certificates each year. In 2026 only a CBAM factor of 2.5% of those embedded emissions is charged, rising on a set schedule to 100% by 2034.

For the non-EU manufacturer, the mechanism operates indirectly. They hold no legal liability to the European Commission. Their EU customers, however, now need granular, production-level emissions data to calculate what they owe. A manufacturer that cannot supply accurate, verifiable data becomes a financial risk its customers cannot quantify, and its goods become harder to place in European supply chains.

The mechanism, and carbon leakage

CBAM is often mistaken for a conventional import tariff designed to raise revenue. A standard tariff is a static percentage applied to the monetary value of a product. CBAM is an environmental border mechanism built to mirror the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS), the internal cap-and-trade market that puts a carbon price on domestic European industry.

The mechanism exists to solve one structural problem: carbon leakage. As the EU raises its internal carbon price to force decarbonisation, domestic producers face higher costs. Without a border mechanism, European buyers would shift sourcing to jurisdictions with weaker climate rules, or EU manufacturers would relocate outside the bloc. Global emissions would be unchanged, and the EU would simply export its industrial base. CBAM neutralises that by applying an equivalent carbon price to imported goods, so the competitive field stays level regardless of origin.

The cost of a CBAM certificate is not fixed. It is tied to the fluctuating auction price of EU ETS allowances, set as a quarterly average in 2026 and moving to a weekly average from 2027. CBAM also targets the greenhouse-gas emissions embedded in the production process itself, not the transport. That includes direct emissions from fuel burned in the facility (Scope 1). The indirect emissions from electricity consumed in production (Scope 2) carry a certificate obligation only for cement and fertilisers in the definitive period; iron and steel, aluminium, and hydrogen are charged on direct emissions only. Emissions from the shipping or freight that carries the goods to the European border sit outside the regulation.

Operational realities in the definitive phase

The move from the transitional phase to the definitive phase in 2026 turned CBAM from a compliance exercise into a financial operation. EU importers now fold dynamic carbon pricing into their procurement models. Because certificate prices track the EU ETS, on a quarterly average in 2026 and a weekly average from 2027, buying them calls for the kind of planning and timing used to hedge currency or commodity risk.

The central constraint is data integrity. Importers must collect emissions data from non-EU facilities, and in the definitive period that data can be used only once verified by an accredited verifier; without it, the marked-up default values apply. A break in the data flow raises the importer's liability directly.

Supply chains for covered goods are reorganising as a result. EU buyers place a premium on suppliers with transparent, digitised emissions tracking. In steel and aluminium, analysts note a "green premium", where non-EU producers who can prove low-emission production command higher prices and longer contracts, while carbon-intensive producers face increasing barriers to entry.

The origin-country carbon-price deduction

CBAM is designed so the obligation can be reduced, or removed entirely, where a carbon price has already been paid in the country of origin. This avoids double taxation and gives third countries a reason to price carbon themselves.

Where a non-EU producer operates under a mandatory carbon tax or an emissions trading system, the embedded emissions of their goods already carry a verifiable domestic carbon cost. The EU importer can declare that effectively paid price and claim a matching reduction in the certificates they must surrender.

Producers in countries with recognised carbon pricing therefore carry a lower certificate cost into Europe. Facilities under the United Kingdom's ETS can deduct the UK carbon price as a third-country carbon price and pass that reduction down to their EU buyers; an agreement to link the UK and EU systems is under negotiation but not yet in force. Goods originating in Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, and Liechtenstein sit outside CBAM entirely, because their carbon pricing systems are linked to or aligned with the EU ETS. Producers in jurisdictions without an equivalent mechanism, or unable to prove the cost was paid, leave their EU importers exposed to the full certificate price.

Where the scope is heading

The perimeter of CBAM is not fixed. The European Commission is mandated to keep reviewing the mechanism's effectiveness and its fit with wider EU climate policy.

The phase-out of free ETS allowances for domestic industry, scheduled to conclude by 2034, is the pacing mechanism for enforcement. As free allowances fall, the border adjustment tightens. A Commission proposal of December 2025 would extend CBAM to around 180 downstream steel and aluminium products from 2028. Organic chemicals and polymers remain candidates for a later extension, to be assessed in the Commission's review due before 1 January 2028. Extending the certificate charge on indirect (Scope 2) electricity emissions to the remaining goods is the subject of a Commission report planned for 2027; if that extension proceeds, manufacturers will need to watch the carbon intensity of their local grid as closely as their direct furnace emissions.

Conclusion

CBAM is a permanent feature of European trade. It converts the carbon embedded in industrial goods into a direct financial liability at the border. For businesses in covered sectors, environmental reporting stopped being a standalone compliance task in 2025. Working under the definitive mechanism connects procurement, supply-chain data, and financial forecasting to a variable European carbon cost.
