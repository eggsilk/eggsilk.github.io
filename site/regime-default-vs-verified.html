Default values versus verified data

Under CBAM an importer either accepts the premium built into default values or gathers verified actual data. The comparison sets the default mark-up against the cost of data systems, supplier coordination, third-party verification, and compliance risk.

In brief

  • The choice between default values and verified data is a decision between accepting a priced premium and paying for supply chain data collection.
  • The cost comparison sets the certificate cost under default values against the combined expense of supplier coordination, internal data systems, third-party verification, and compliance risk.
  • Default values are set from the exporting country's average emission intensity for each good, plus a published mark-up; only where no reliable country data exists does a fallback apply, based on the ten highest-intensity exporting countries for that good. Importers who cannot produce verifiable actual emissions data pay the mark-up on every tonne.

The penalty of the fallback mechanism

As of 1 January 2026, the European Union's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is in its definitive period. Financial obligations are active, and the lenient reporting standards of the transitional phase, which ended in December 2025, no longer apply. Importers must now surrender certificates corresponding to the embedded emissions of their imported steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, and hydrogen.

The European Commission provides default values for importers who cannot obtain actual emissions data from their non-EU suppliers. A common misconception treats these default values as a simple, permanent alternative to gathering actual data. The regulatory architecture dictates otherwise. Default values function as a fallback mechanism that carries a deliberate premium over measured data.

The Commission calculates these values conservatively. They are based on the average emission intensity of the exporting country for a given good. On top of that country-average baseline, default values carry a mark-up applied to incentivise the use of actual data: for aluminium, cement and iron and steel, 10% in 2026 rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers. An importer relying on defaults pays that premium on every tonne of imported goods for which no data is produced.

The true cost stack of actual data

Calculating the cost difference between default values and actual data is not as simple as comparing the volume of required certificates against the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) allowance price. Acquiring "verified actual emissions data" is a capital-intensive operational process.

The total cost of using actual data includes upgrading Enterprise Resource Planning (ERP) systems to track granular emissions across individual shipments. It requires allocating internal labour to collate reports and manage supplier relations. The definitive period mandates that actual emissions data be validated by accredited third-party verifiers. Verifier fees scale with the complexity of the supply chain and the number of production installations involved.

Industry analyses consistently find that these administrative and verification costs, spanning data collection, supplier coordination, and third-party auditing, form a substantial share of the total compliance burden, concentrated in sectors like steel. For an importer sourcing small volumes from dozens of different non-EU suppliers, the fixed administrative and verification costs of securing actual data frequently eclipse the savings generated by requiring fewer CBAM certificates.

Furthermore, the CBAM regulation allows importers to reduce their certificate liability by claiming a credit for any carbon price already paid in the country of origin. Claiming this deduction strictly requires verifiable actual data. Using default values complicates or entirely negates an importer's ability to claim this cost reduction, further widening the financial gap between the two approaches for goods originating in jurisdictions with localised carbon pricing.

The supplier capability bottleneck

The decision to use actual data rarely rests entirely on the EU importer's internal cost-benefit analysis. The ability to avoid default values is entirely dependent on the non-EU manufacturer's capacity to measure, allocate, and verify emissions at the installation level.

If a supplier cannot or will not implement the necessary carbon accounting infrastructure, the importer is forced to declare default values. This dynamic shifts the real challenge from internal compliance to supplier negotiation and capacity-building. Large importers possess the buying power to mandate data compliance from their suppliers as a condition of future contracts. Small and medium-sized enterprises frequently lack this leverage, leaving them to absorb the inflated cost of default certificates because their suppliers will not shoulder the burden of verification.

It is also an error to assume a producer's actual emissions will always be lower than the default values. While defaults are set high by design, a producer using highly carbon-intensive energy sources or inefficient manufacturing processes may discover their actual footprint exceeds the Commission's benchmark. Measurement is the only way to establish the baseline.

Compliance risk and the volatility factor

Declaring actual emissions carries its own compliance liability. The definitive period sets penalties for misreporting and for failing to surrender sufficient certificates. The penalty is €100 per tonne of embedded emissions (harmonised with the EU ETS excess-emissions penalty, inflation-indexed), and the importer must still buy and surrender the missing certificates.

Because verification relies on complex carbon accounting frameworks, errors in data collection expose the importer to audits and reputational damage. For some importers, particularly those doubtful of their suppliers' data integrity, default values function as a costly but legally secure safe harbour. Paying the inflated default rate removes the risk of audit penalties related to misreported installation data.

This risk calculation is further complicated by the fluctuating price of the certificates themselves. The cost of a CBAM certificate is pegged to a quarterly average of EU ETS auction prices in 2026, moving to a weekly average from 2027. Over the preceding years, EUA prices frequently traded between €60 and €90 per tonne, influenced by energy markets and industrial output. Because the absolute cost difference between using defaults and actuals changes weekly alongside the ETS price, long-term forecasting requires managing active carbon market exposure.

The opportunity cost of defaults

Relying on default values leaves the importer without emissions data on its own supply chain. Gathering verified actual data requires upfront investment, and the resulting data has uses beyond the CBAM declaration itself.

Importers with actual emissions data can compare suppliers by emissions intensity and use that comparison in procurement decisions. Non-EU manufacturers who provide verified data avoid the default premium that applies at the border to goods declared without it.

The bottom line

The financial difference between default values and verified actual data under CBAM cannot be calculated by looking at certificate prices alone. It requires weighing the mark-up built into default values against the operational expense, supplier constraints, and compliance risk of gathering verified data. Defaults carry a published mark-up; verified data carries collection and verification costs.
