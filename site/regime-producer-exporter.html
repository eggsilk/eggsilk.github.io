CBAM regime · The players

The non-EU producer and exporter

CBAM places its legal cost on the EU importer rather than the non-EU producer. The producer's verified emissions data sets the amount its EU customer pays, so a manufacturer that cannot supply granular, third-party verified figures becomes a more expensive supplier than one that can.

In brief

  • The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism does not tax non-EU producers directly; the EU importer pays a variable cost based on the producer's emissions data.
  • If a producer does not provide granular, third-party verified emissions data, the EU importer must apply default values set from the exporting country's average emission intensity for the good, plus a published mark-up.
  • Producers that establish plant-specific measurement systems and document local carbon payments lower the certificate cost their EU customers pay.

The commercial reality of importer liability

Since the definitive phase of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) began on 1 January 2026, every tonne of cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertiliser, electricity, and hydrogen entering the European Union carries a variable financial liability. CBAM is neither a direct tax on the non-EU manufacturer nor a standard customs tariff on its goods.

The primary legal and financial obligation falls entirely on the authorised CBAM declarant, which is typically the EU importer. The importer must purchase and surrender CBAM certificates for the embedded carbon emissions of the imported goods, though in 2026 only the CBAM factor of 2.5% of those emissions is charged, rising per schedule to 100% by 2034. The price of these certificates fluctuates with the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) allowance price, set as a quarterly average in 2026 and a weekly average from 2027.

While the legal burden rests with the importer, the commercial burden rests entirely with the non-EU producer. Importers cannot calculate their financial liability without precise, verified data from the manufacturing facility. A non-EU manufacturer's ability to maintain access to the European market therefore depends directly on the quality and availability of its product-level emissions data. If a producer cannot supply this data, the importer faces an unquantifiable financial risk and has reason to shift its supply chain to a manufacturer that can.

The cost of default values

During the transitional period that ended on 31 December 2025, importers reported emissions without financial consequence, often relying on estimated or default data when suppliers lacked internal measurement systems. As of 2026, reported emissions carry a financial cost.

When an importer cannot provide actual, verified embedded emissions data from a non-EU producer, Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621 mandates the use of default values. The European Commission designed these figures to be punitive: they are calculated from the average emission intensity of the exporting country for that specific good. On top of that country-average baseline, default values carry a mark-up applied to incentivise the use of actual data: for aluminium, cement and iron and steel, 10% in 2026 rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers.

For specific industrial goods, applying these default values raises the certificate cost. For efficient producers in sectors like steel and cement, default values regularly price embedded carbon well above actual plant emissions. A Turkish steel producer exporting to the EU, for example, either invests in the systems required to prove its specific emissions profile, or its EU customer pays a premium based on the average carbon intensity of Turkish steel production plus the published mark-up; only where no reliable country-level data exists does the default fall back on the average of the ten highest-intensity exporting countries. Goods priced on default values carry a higher certificate cost than identical goods priced on verified actual data.

The operational data mandate

Providing usable data requires changes in how non-EU facilities track their operations. Standard corporate Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) reports and national-level environmental disclosures are insufficient for CBAM compliance. The regulation demands granular accounting of embedded emissions at the installation level, tied strictly to specific Harmonized System (HS) product codes.

The detailed EU methodology requires plant-specific calculations for direct emissions resulting from the production process (Scope 1) and specific indirect emissions tied to the electricity consumed during manufacturing (Scope 2). Broader supply chain emissions, such as raw material transport or employee travel (Scope 3), are excluded.

Manufacturers must adopt activity-based monitoring. This involves tracking precise quantities of fuel consumption and raw material inputs, and multiplying them by specific emission factors derived from laboratory analyses or mass balance equations. For indirect emissions, producers must trace the actual emissions factor of the electricity consumed at the facility; if this cannot be verified, strict grid-average default values apply.

Any data submitted for the definitive period must be verified by an independent, accredited third-party verifier. Facilities that spent the 2023 to 2025 transitional period establishing internal Measurement, Reporting, and Verification (MRV) systems patterned after the EU ETS are now capable of passing these audits. Facilities attempting to extract this data retroactively from high-level corporate footprint reports are discovering that the data architecture required by the implementing regulation does not exist in their current systems.

Deducting carbon prices paid locally

CBAM contains a mechanism to prevent double charging for the same carbon. If an effective carbon price has already been paid in the country of origin, the EU importer can deduct that amount from its final CBAM certificate liability, which lowers the final cost of the goods in Europe.

An "effective carbon price" refers to a mandatory charge imposed under a national or regional carbon pricing system, such as a direct carbon tax or an active Emissions Trading System. Voluntary offsetting schemes and government subsidies do not qualify. For example, producers in Turkey, whose Climate Law No. 7552 (July 2025) establishes a national ETS with a pilot phase running 2026 to 2027, will operate within a jurisdiction that levies an explicit price on carbon. During the pilot phase, however, allowances are allocated largely free of charge, so the deductible effective carbon price is small to nil once free allocation is netted off, as the regulation requires; the deduction becomes material when auctioning begins in the full phase from 2028.

Claiming this deduction requires extensive documentation. The non-EU producer must provide the EU importer with an auditable paper trail proving the legal basis of the local scheme, the exact amount paid per tonne of CO₂ equivalent, and official receipts or audited financial statements from the relevant national authority. The producer's internal accounting must also explicitly link the carbon price paid to the specific embedded emissions of the exact HS-coded goods exported to the EU.

Any rebates, free allowances, or refunds received by the producer in its home country must be transparently accounted for and subtracted from the deductible amount. When executed correctly, a manufacturer operating in a jurisdiction with a verifiable carbon price can pass those documented savings directly to its European buyer; a competitor in a region without recognised carbon pricing, or without organised compliance data, cannot offer the same deduction.

The bottom line

A non-EU manufacturer's exposure to the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism is measured entirely by its data architecture. The legal burden to purchase certificates falls on the European importer, but the importer relies wholly on the manufacturer to avoid marked-up default values. By verifying actual installation-level emissions and documenting local carbon taxes paid, a producer determines the certificate cost its European buyer faces, and with it the final landed cost of its products.
