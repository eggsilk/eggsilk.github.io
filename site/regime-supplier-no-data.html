CBAM regime · Through the supply chain

My supplier cannot give verified data

When a non-EU supplier fails to provide verified emissions data, the EU importer is forced onto default values that raise its certificate bill. The importer carries sole legal responsibility, so the fix sits in procurement rather than in a compliance workaround.

In brief

  • Missing supplier emissions data raises the importer's certificate bill directly; the remedy sits in procurement rather than in compliance workarounds.
  • EU importers bear sole legal responsibility as Authorised CBAM Declarants and must apply default values, set above most facilities' actual emissions, if actual data is absent.
  • Resolving the issue requires diagnosing whether a supplier lacks technical capability or commercial willingness, then applying financial incentives or diversifying supply.

The financial mechanics of missing data

As of 1 January 2026, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism operates in its definitive period. The transitional phase of reporting-only obligations has concluded, and financial liabilities are fully active. EU importers of covered goods now function as Authorised CBAM Declarants, meaning they are legally responsible for purchasing and surrendering certificates corresponding to the embedded emissions of their imports.

When a third-country supplier fails to provide emissions data, the importer does not get a reprieve from filing. The regulatory obligation is absolute. Instead, the importer is forced to use the default emissions values published by the European Commission.

These default values are set deliberately high. They are conservative estimates that sit above the actual average emissions of most production facilities. The calculation methodology relies on the average emissions intensity of the specific product in the country of origin. If reliable country-level data is unavailable, the Commission bases the default value on the average emission intensity of the ten exporting countries with the highest intensities for which reliable data exists for that type of goods, under Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2621.

Using default values guarantees the importer pays a premium. A foreign producer operating a highly efficient, low-emission facility is treated identically to a heavy polluter if the data is missing. The cost of missing supplier data manifests as a higher volume of CBAM certificates the importer must purchase.

Diagnosing the supplier deficit

Fixing the data gap requires diagnosing the root cause. A supplier failing to deliver verified data generally falls into one of two categories: they either lack the technical capability, or they lack the commercial willingness.

A capability failure occurs when a supplier operates in a jurisdiction with no domestic carbon pricing mechanism or mandatory environmental reporting framework. These suppliers often lack the internal expertise to monitor, calculate, and verify embedded emissions according to the rigid methodologies dictated by the European Commission. For sole-source suppliers facing this barrier, importers typically intervene directly. Importers secure data by funding independent emissions audits, deploying external consultants to the supplier's facilities, and integrating carbon accounting software into the supplier's operations. The cost of this capacity building is routinely lower than the cumulative cost of relying on default values over multiple import cycles.

A willingness failure is a commercial stand-off. The supplier possesses the resources to calculate emissions but views the administrative burden as a low priority, or they refuse to disclose operational data for competitive reasons. This requires commercial leverage rather than technical support.

Industry practitioners at firms such as PwC and EY treat CBAM compliance as a procurement function rather than a regulatory filing exercise. When a supplier refuses to provide data, the solution lies in redefining the terms of trade. EU importers are increasingly categorising suppliers by their "CBAM-readiness," shifting order volumes away from opaque suppliers and toward producers capable of delivering verified data alongside their physical goods.

Embedding compliance in procurement

Procurement contracts govern supplier behaviour. In the definitive period, EU importers are insulating themselves from default values by rewriting these agreements to shift the financial risk back to the supplier.

Contracts now treat verified emissions data as a contractual deliverable, subject to the same deadlines and quality standards as the physical commodity. If a supplier delivers steel on time but fails to deliver the emissions data, the delivery is contractually incomplete.

To enforce this, importers implement specific pricing adjustments and indemnification clauses. If a supplier's failure to provide accurate data forces the EU importer to use expensive default values, the contract dictates that the supplier must reimburse the importer for the excess certificate costs. Similarly, purchase agreements are increasingly structured with dynamic pricing, where the final invoice amount is adjusted based on the embedded emissions intensity. Lower actual emissions translate to a higher purchase price for the supplier. This gives the supplier a direct financial incentive to monitor and report accurate figures.

Importers also write audit rights into these agreements, allowing third-party inspectors to access the supplier's production facilities to verify the underlying calculation methodologies.

The accreditation standard

Procuring the data is only half the requirement; the data must also meet EU verification standards. A common misstep for importers is accepting an emissions report stamped by a supplier's local consulting firm, assuming any third-party verification satisfies the regulatory requirement.

CBAM regulations mandate that an independent verifier review the embedded emissions data. This verifier must be accredited by a national accreditation body operating under Regulation (EC) No 765/2008 and subject to peer evaluation within the European co-operation for Accreditation (EA).

The Authorised CBAM Declarant carries the liability for confirming that the verifier meets these criteria. Accepting invalid verification nullifies the actual data; the default values then apply, and the importer faces penalties for inaccurate declarations. Importers must cross-reference the supplier's chosen verifier with the EA's directory of recognised national accreditation bodies before accepting the data package.

Enforcement and the cost of inaction

The penalties for failure in the definitive period accumulate. If an importer is forced to use default values but subsequently fails to surrender the correct, higher volume of CBAM certificates by the 30 September annual deadline, the financial consequences compound.

The baseline penalty for failing to surrender sufficient CBAM certificates is €100 per tonne of unreported emissions, indexed to the European consumer price index. This €100 fine does not cancel the underlying obligation; the importer must pay the penalty and still purchase the missing certificates. Furthermore, national competent authorities within the EU Member States possess the mandate to levy additional, dissuasive fines for submitting incorrect declarations or failing to ensure proper verification.

An importer that treats supplier data acquisition as a passive process pays the default premium on every import cycle. Commercial contracts, sourcing decisions, and supplier support are the instruments through which verified carbon data is secured.

The bottom line

Missing supplier data legally forces an EU importer to use default emissions values, increasing the volume of CBAM certificates they must buy. Because the importer bears sole legal and financial responsibility for the declaration, resolving the data gap requires procurement contracts, indemnification clauses, and supplier support that make accurate carbon reporting a contractual condition of supply.
