CBAM regime · The players

The EU importer and authorised declarant

For most operations the EU importer and the authorised CBAM declarant are the same entity, fixed by the structure of the company's customs representation. Since the definitive period began, that role carries a direct financial liability priced against EU carbon allowances.

In brief

  • The identity of the CBAM declarant is fixed by customs representation agreements, which link the EU importer directly to the compliance liabilities.
  • As of 1 January 2026, CBAM changed from a reporting exercise into a direct operational cost requiring the purchase of emissions certificates.
  • Compliance relies on extracting verifiable emissions data from non-EU suppliers to avoid the marked-up cost of default values.

The legal mechanics of the declarant role

For the vast majority of operations, the EU importer and the authorised CBAM declarant are the same entity. Which entity holds the role follows from the structure of the company's customs representation.

Under Regulation (EU) 2023/956, the declarant is the person lodging a customs declaration for release for free circulation in their own name, or the person on whose behalf that declaration is lodged. If an EU importer manages its own customs or uses a direct customs representative, the importer acts as the declarant and holds the legal liability for CBAM compliance. Only when an importer uses an indirect customs representative does the representative formally assume the declarant role and the associated liabilities.

The status of authorised CBAM declarant is a formal legal designation; it is not conferred automatically on a company because it imports covered goods such as steel, cement, or aluminium. Eligible entities intending to act as the declarant must actively apply for this status through the CBAM Registry to their national competent authority, such as the DEHSt in Germany or the DGDDI in France. Operating and importing covered goods without this formal authorisation during the definitive period constitutes a direct compliance failure.

The shift from reporting to direct financial liability

CBAM was introduced in two phases with different operational requirements. The transitional period, which ran from 1 October 2023 to 31 December 2025, functioned solely as a data-gathering exercise. Importers filed quarterly reports detailing embedded emissions, but no financial penalties or certificate purchases were attached to the carbon footprint of the goods.

As of 1 January 2026, the definitive period is fully active and CBAM operates as a financial mechanism at the border. The core obligation requires the authorised declarant to purchase and surrender CBAM certificates for the verified embedded CO₂e brought into the EU. In 2026, though, only the CBAM factor of 2.5% of those embedded emissions is charged, rising per schedule to 100% by 2034.

The financial exposure is variable. The price of a CBAM certificate is pegged to a quarterly average of EU ETS auction prices in 2026, moving to a weekly average from 2027. For the first quarter of 2026, that calculation set the official certificate price at €75.36 per tonne of CO₂e, a figure that moves with the auction market each quarter. Certificate costs are therefore a matter for corporate finance and procurement departments, not only for customs teams. Importers must manage dedicated budgets so that they can acquire the necessary volume of certificates ahead of the annual surrender deadline. The first such deadline, covering imports made throughout 2026, falls on 30 September 2027.

The dependency on non-EU data supply chains

Under the mechanics of the definitive period, an EU importer's financial liability is calculated from data generated by third parties outside of European jurisdiction. The total cost of certificates surrendered by 30 September each year depends on the specific, verified embedded emissions of the imported goods.

Obtaining this data is the primary operational hurdle of the definitive period. Importers must extract granular methodologies detailing direct and indirect emissions, production volumes, and specific manufacturing processes from their non-EU producers. From 2026 onward, this data must be verified by an independent, accredited verifier before it is submitted to the European Commission.

When non-EU suppliers fail to provide verifiable data, the regulation mandates that the importer apply default values published by the Commission. These default values are explicitly designed to be punitive. Based on the average emission intensity of the exporting country for a given good, the defaults penalise non-reporting and prevent data obscurity from acting as a cost-saving measure. On top of that, default values carry a mark-up applied to incentivise the use of actual data: for aluminium, cement and iron and steel, 10% in 2026 rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers. Relying on default values therefore increases the number of certificates an importer must buy.

Operating within the definitive registry

The operational hub for executing these obligations is the definitive CBAM Registry, which went live at the beginning of 2026 to replace the transitional reporting platform. Authorised declarants use this central software infrastructure to process their initial authorisation applications, monitor their financial balances for certificate acquisitions, and file the annual declarations.

Because the system handles the exchange of data with accredited verifiers and the eventual surrender of financial certificates, the registry demands high data accuracy. Importers are establishing internal governance teams that bridge logistics, procurement, and finance. Logistics data triggers the initial customs liability, procurement manages the supplier contracts necessary to enforce data-sharing agreements, and finance controls the capital required to purchase certificates within the registry's interface.

Cost mitigation

Because the definitive phase imposes a variable carbon price on specific material imports, the certificate cost can be managed as well as reported. Tracking the price of EU ETS allowances allows finance teams to forecast expected certificate costs and allocate financial reserves.

Beyond forecasting certificate prices, structural mitigation involves the supply chain itself. Some importers fund or incentivise decarbonisation at the non-EU facility level. Because the CBAM liability is calculated per tonne of embedded carbon, lowering the actual emissions of the manufacturing process translates directly to fewer required certificates. This reduces the importer's cost at the border and its exposure to movements in the EU ETS carbon price.

The bottom line

Acting as the authorised CBAM declarant is a legal designation that binds the EU importer to financial liabilities, contingent upon its customs representation. Now that the definitive period is active, the core operational requirement is purchasing emissions certificates tied to the price of EU carbon allowances. Avoiding the default-value premium requires a data supply chain that can extract and verify actual emissions figures from non-EU manufacturers.
