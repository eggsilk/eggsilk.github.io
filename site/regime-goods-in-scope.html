CBAM regime · Scope and obligations

Which of my imported goods fall under CBAM?

CBAM applicability is set by the 8-digit Combined Nomenclature code an import carries, matched against Annex I of the regulation. Identifying a covered good is only the first step. The real obligation is securing verified, installation-level emissions data from the non-EU producer.

In brief

  • CBAM is an emissions accounting regime assigned to customs, not a static tariff applied to generic product categories.
  • Applicability is determined exclusively by 8-digit Combined Nomenclature (CN) codes in Annex I of Regulation (EU) 2023/956.
  • Since January 2026, failing to secure installation-level supplier emissions data triggers marked-up default values and statutory financial penalties.

The data problem disguised as customs

The definitive phase of the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) entered into force on 1 January 2026. The transitional reporting period, which ended on 31 December 2025, served as a pilot phase. As of mid-2026, EU importers of covered goods operate under legal obligations to purchase and surrender CBAM certificates that correspond to the embedded emissions of their imports.

Treating CBAM applicability as a simple product identification exercise misreads the regulation. The mechanism taxes the tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e) embedded in goods during their manufacturing process, not the physical goods themselves.

An identical shipment of steel wire from two different non-EU suppliers therefore carries two different compliance profiles. The financial liability is set by the carbon intensity of the specific production facility, not the product's name. The core operational task for an authorised declarant is a continuous data pipeline that secures, verifies, and reports granular emissions metrics from every non-EU production installation in the supply chain, in addition to identifying covered goods at the border.

Classification mechanics and statutory overrides

The legal authority determining which goods fall under CBAM is entirely contained within Annex I of the CBAM Regulation (EU) 2023/956. Applicability is determined exclusively by the 8-digit Combined Nomenclature (CN) code used on the customs declaration.

The regulation targets six broad, carbon-intensive categories: iron and steel, cement, aluminium, fertilisers, hydrogen, and electricity. Commercial product descriptions carry no legal weight. Common terms like "steel pipes" or "aluminium framing" are insufficient for compliance. If the specific 8-digit CN code assigned to the import matches a code listed in Annex I, the consignment is subject to CBAM provisions.

Identifying the correct CN code is only the initial threshold. Several statutory conditions override the product classification and can alter or eliminate the obligation entirely:

  • Origin: Goods originating from countries within the European Economic Area (Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) and Switzerland are exempt from the mechanism due to their participation in or linkage to the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS).
  • De-minimis mass: An importer is exempt if its total mass of covered goods stays below a 50-tonne annual threshold, a rule designed to alleviate the administrative burden on minor volumes. This exemption does not apply to electricity or hydrogen.
  • Customs procedure: The physical entry of goods into the EU does not automatically trigger liability. Goods imported under the "inward processing" procedure, meaning they are brought into the EU temporarily for manufacturing or repair and subsequently re-exported, are not subject to CBAM.

The supplier data bottleneck

Confirming a product falls under CBAM initiates the actual compliance hurdle: obtaining verified, installation-level embedded emissions data from the non-EU producer.

This requirement exposes the primary operational bottleneck for EU declarants in 2026. Without primary data calculated according to the EU methodology, importers are forced to rely on default values established by the European Commission. These default values are set from the average emission intensity of the exporting country for that good, plus a published mark-up; where reliable country-level data is unavailable, the fallback is the average of the ten exporting countries with the highest emission intensity. In either case they increase the number of CBAM certificates an importer must purchase and surrender.

Securing primary data requires detailed disclosure from non-EU supply chains. The regulation demands distinct accounting for direct emissions and, in some categories, indirect emissions (Scope 2 emissions from electricity consumption).

Industry associations translate these requirements into sector-level guidance. Eurofer (the European Steel Association) and the European Aluminium association provide detailed methodological frameworks for their respective sectors, accounting for complexities like scrap metal inputs and secondary production. For cement, existing frameworks established by the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD) Cement Sustainability Initiative, such as the "Getting the Numbers Right" database, provide a foundation for calculating emissions that align with EU rules.

Readiness among non-EU suppliers varies widely by region and firm size. In Turkey, alignment efforts are underway: industry associations have subsidised training on emissions calculation and data verification for their steel and cement exporters, and Turkey is working to align its national emissions trading system with the EU ETS.

In China, a major exporter of steel and aluminium, readiness varies by firm size. Large industrial producers invest in precise data collection systems and engage consultants to assess embedded emissions, often supported by local government initiatives. Reliance on Indian and smaller Chinese suppliers carries more risk for EU importers, as many small-to-medium enterprises lack the internal systems to calculate embedded emissions to the standard required by the Implementing Regulation (EU) 2025/2547.

Enforcement realities and planned expansion

The financial consequences for failing to manage this data pipeline are in force. For the definitive period, importers must surrender the required number of CBAM certificates by 30 September of each year for the emissions generated by imports in the preceding calendar year; the first surrender, covering 2026 imports, is due on 30 September 2027.

Failing to surrender sufficient certificates triggers a baseline penalty of €100 per tonne of unreported embedded emissions. This penalty is adjusted annually for inflation and is levied in addition to the cost of purchasing the missing certificates. National competent authorities within EU Member States execute enforcement. In cases of persistent non-compliance, these authorities hold the statutory power to revoke a declarant's authorisation, which prohibits the entity from importing any CBAM-covered goods into the European Union.

The current list of covered goods is also temporary. The regulation is explicitly designed to expand. The European Commission is mandated to submit a comprehensive review report to the European Parliament and the Council before 1 January 2028.

This review will assess the feasibility of extending the mechanism to other goods deemed at risk of carbon leakage, specifically targeting organic chemicals and polymers. The review will also evaluate incorporating indirect emissions across all covered categories, which would increase the data requirements for sectors like steel and aluminium where Scope 2 emissions are currently excluded or partially measured. Both the product scope and the depth of the emissions accounting requirements are expected to grow over time.

The bottom line

Determining CBAM coverage requires precise mapping of 8-digit CN codes against Annex I of the regulation, factoring in exemptions for origin, mass, and customs procedures. Identifying the goods, however, merely exposes the financial liability. The operational reality for EU importers in the definitive period is managing the cost of that liability by securing verified, installation-specific emissions data from non-EU suppliers to avoid the default-value premium and statutory fines.
