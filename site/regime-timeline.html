The CBAM timeline

The CBAM timeline is a financial phase-in trajectory locked to the phase-out of free EU ETS allowances for domestic producers. It runs from a nominal liability in 2026 to full liability in 2034, but obligations begin with the product code, not the calendar.

In brief

  • The CBAM timeline is a financial phase-in trajectory dictated entirely by the phase-out of free EU ETS allowances for domestic producers.
  • Statutory dates remain irrelevant unless an importer's specific goods match the exact Combined Nomenclature codes targeted by the regulation.
  • Cost-free reliance on default emissions data ended with the transitional period; in the definitive period defaults remain available without limit, but carry a published mark-up on the certificate requirement.

The applicability gate

The Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) imposes an escalating financial timeline, but obligations begin with product classification, not a calendar date. For an EU importer or a non-EU manufacturer supplying the bloc, the initial point of failure is misunderstanding scope.

Applicability hinges entirely on the exact Combined Nomenclature (CN) codes of the imported goods and their country of origin. The mechanism targets specific carbon-intensive sectors: iron and steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen, and electricity. If an imported good does not match a listed CN code, or if it originates from an exempt jurisdiction like Switzerland or Norway, the CBAM timeline does not apply. The phase-in schedule sets the depth of the financial cost for covered goods; it does not expand the product scope over time.

The transitional period in retrospect

The transitional period, which ran from October 2023 to December 2025, functioned as a mandatory data-gathering exercise rather than a grace period. Importers were required to submit quarterly reports detailing both the quantity of imported goods and their direct and indirect embedded greenhouse gas emissions. No financial payments were due.

During this phase, the European Commission permitted the use of default values to estimate emissions when actual supplier data was unavailable. This leniency accommodated the initial lack of carbon accounting infrastructure among non-EU producers. The transitional period exposed these informational gaps. Importers who treated the phase as a reporting exercise, rather than the period in which to build IT integrations and obtain supply-chain data, entered the definitive period without that infrastructure.

The definitive period and the phase-in schedule

On 1 January 2026, CBAM entered its definitive period, activating the mechanism's financial obligations. Importers must now operate as "authorised CBAM declarants" and are liable for certificates corresponding to the embedded emissions of their goods. The liability accrues from 1 January 2026, but certificate sales begin on 1 February 2027, with the first purchases covering 2026 imports retroactively. The first annual declaration for the 2026 operating year, along with the required certificate surrender, is due by 30 September 2027 (extended from 31 May by the Omnibus).

The defining feature of this period is the phase-in schedule. The schedule is locked to the phase-out of free carbon allowances allocated to domestic EU producers under the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS). As free allowances for European industry decrease, the financial obligation for importers increases proportionately.

The European Commission defines this as the "CBAM coefficient", which sets the exact percentage of embedded emissions for which an importer must surrender certificates. The trajectory from 2026 to 2034 follows a specific, accelerating schedule:

  • 2026: Importers are liable for 2.5% of their embedded emissions.
  • 2027: Liability increases to 5%.
  • 2028: Liability reaches 10%.
  • 2029: Liability rises to 22.5%.
  • 2030: Liability rises to 48.5%.
  • 2031: Liability rises to 61%.
  • 2032: Liability reaches 73.5%.
  • 2033: Liability rises to 86%.
  • 2034: Free EU ETS allowances drop to zero, and CBAM liability reaches 100%.

The price of a CBAM certificate is tied to a quarterly average of EU ETS auction prices in 2026, moving to a weekly average from 2027. Because the liability percentage compounds with the fluctuating market price of carbon, forecasting the actual cash impact requires modelling both the statutory phase-in coefficient and projected ETS trading prices.

Operational realities and the cost of non-compliance

The definitive period alters the risk profile for importers. The European Commission expects declarations to be based on actual, verified emissions data obtained from the original manufacturing facilities. Default values remain available without limit in the definitive period, but carry a published mark-up on top of country-average figures: 10% in 2026 rising to 30% by 2028 for cement, iron and steel, and aluminium, and 1% for fertilisers.

Non-compliance carries financial penalties. The penalty for failing to surrender sufficient CBAM certificates aligns precisely with the ETS penalty for excess emissions: €100 per tonne of unreported carbon, adjusted for inflation. This €100 per tonne fine is levied in addition to the requirement to surrender the missing certificates, effectively doubling the cost of an error.

The administrative overhead of managing these obligations has pushed importers toward enterprise resource planning (ERP) integrations. Tracking specific CN codes, validating non-EU supplier emissions data, distinguishing between direct and indirect (Scope 2) emissions, and calculating the corresponding certificate requirements is difficult to sustain manually.

Global carbon pricing and sourcing strategy

The definitive period gives non-EU nations a reason to implement their own carbon pricing systems. Under CBAM rules, if a carbon price has already been paid in the country of origin, the importer can claim a proportional reduction in the number of CBAM certificates they must surrender.

In response to the January 2026 activation, major exporting nations are shifting domestic policies. Turkey is moving forward with a pilot ETS to capture carbon revenue domestically rather than ceding it to the European Union. China is integrating its steel and cement sectors into its national ETS, establishing a verifiable carbon price that Chinese exporters can use to offset EU liabilities. India continues to weigh domestic carbon taxes against WTO challenges.

For EU importers, these geopolitical shifts dictate sourcing strategy. Procurement decisions now require calculating the net carbon liability of a supplier, which means factoring in the origin country's domestic carbon tax or ETS price, the supplier's actual emissions efficiency, and the escalating EU phase-in coefficient.

Conclusion

The CBAM timeline is an active financial mechanism that dictates the escalating cost of importing carbon-intensive goods into the European Union. From a nominal 2.5% liability in 2026 to total exposure in 2034, the cost trajectory mirrors the exact phase-out of domestic ETS allowances. Forecasting the cost for a given importer requires three inputs: the phase-in coefficient, the market price of carbon, and the verifiable emissions data of non-EU suppliers.
