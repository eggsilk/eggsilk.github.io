What counts as embedded emissions

Under CBAM, embedded emissions are a product-specific calculation set by the exact good imported, its production route, and defined system boundaries, rather than a single universal figure. What an importer pays turns on whether it can supply verifiable data rather than fall back on marked-up EU default values.

In brief

  • CBAM liability is determined by an importer's ability to provide verifiable, product-specific production data.
  • Without accredited third-party verification, importers default to EU baseline values, which carry a financial markup of 10% in 2026 for cement, iron and steel, and aluminium, rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers.
  • Calculations follow defined boundaries: indirect emissions mean only electricity, and precursors are limited to a set list, rather than a full life-cycle assessment.

The operational reality of embedded emissions

On 1 January 2026, the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) shifted from a reporting exercise to a binding financial obligation. EU importers of covered goods, namely cement, iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers, electricity, and hydrogen, must now operate as authorised declarants and purchase CBAM certificates to cover their imported emissions.

Under this definitive period, "embedded emissions" is a product-specific calculation determined by the exact good imported (categorised by its Combined Nomenclature or CN code), its specific production route, and the system boundaries set out in Regulation (EU) 2025/2547.

The central compliance mechanism governing CBAM is data quality. Importers lacking installation-specific, verified data from non-EU manufacturers are forced to use EU default values. These default values represent country-average emission intensities, and they carry a systematic markup designed to incentivise the use of actual data. For aluminium, cement and iron and steel this markup is 10% in 2026, rising to 30% by 2028; for fertilisers it is 1%.

Relying on these default values carries a measurable cost. For a steel mill emitting 1.5 tonnes of CO₂ equivalent per tonne of product, defaulting to a 2.1 tCO₂e baseline at the first quarter 2026 carbon clearing price of €75.36 inflates the certificate cost. The financial liability is determined by the gap between verifiable actual emissions and these marked-up default baselines.

Direct and indirect emissions boundaries

The classification of emissions under CBAM mirrors the EU Emissions Trading System (ETS) to ensure parity between domestic European producers and foreign manufacturers. The scope of covered greenhouse gases remains limited to carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrous oxide (N2O), and perfluorocarbons (PFCs) for aluminium production. Methane is excluded.

Direct emissions are those released within the physical boundaries of the production facility. This covers both process emissions generated by chemical reactions, such as the calcination of limestone in cement production, and combustion emissions from fuel used to generate heat. Operators must determine these figures using the specific monitoring principles outlined in Annex II of the implementing regulation.

Under CBAM, indirect emissions mean exclusively the emissions generated from the production of electricity consumed during the manufacturing process, occurring outside the facility. They are narrower than standard corporate Scope 2 and Scope 3 reporting, which encompasses transportation, employee travel, and broad supply chain activities.

Financial liability for these indirect emissions currently varies by sector. For cement and fertilisers, importers must surrender certificates for both direct and indirect emissions. For iron, steel, aluminium, and hydrogen, financial obligations are currently restricted to direct emissions, though the European Commission will review potential expansion of indirect emissions coverage across these remaining sectors in 2027.

The precursor list versus life-cycle assessment

CBAM measures emissions within defined system boundaries that include specific "precursors", the energy-intensive intermediate materials named in the regulation. The calculation is narrower than a life-cycle assessment and does not cover the entire carbon footprint of a product from cradle to grave.

Emissions from early upstream raw materials, such as iron ore mining or bauxite extraction, are excluded from the calculation. Only designated precursors trigger embedded emissions reporting. For cement, clinker is the primary precursor whose emissions must be included. For complex fertilisers, ammonia is the regulated precursor. The iron and steel sector requires data on inputs like pig iron, sponge iron, or specific steel products used to manufacture downstream goods like nuts and bolts. Primary aluminium generally involves no precursor inputs under the regulation, so its system boundaries are simpler than those of complex steel goods.

If a non-EU manufacturer uses a covered precursor but cannot determine its origin or acquire verifiable emissions data for it, the calculation falls back to conservative default values for that specific precursor. This compounds the financial risk for complex goods, where data gaps deep in the supply chain cascade into higher final certificate requirements for the importer.

Mandatory verification and data acquisition

To use actual emissions data and avoid default values, EU importers must attach a verification report to their annual CBAM declaration, the first of which is due by 30 September 2027, for 2026 imports. This requires engaging an independent verification body accredited by an EU National Accreditation Body (under the Regulation (EC) No 765/2008 framework) against the harmonised standard EN ISO/IEC 17029:2019.

For the 2026 verification period, a physical on-site visit to every non-EU production installation is mandatory. Verifiers review the installation's monitoring plan, evaluate data sampling methods, and confirm that the accounting of heat, electricity, and waste gas flows strictly aligns with EU ETS methodologies. Only from 2027 onward will virtual visits be permitted under specific low-risk conditions, though physical visits will still be required at least once every two years.

National Accreditation Bodies have begun processing applications; the first accreditations are expected in the autumn of 2026, and none had been granted as of mid-2026. Engaging a verifier costs an estimated €5,000 to €50,000 per installation. Given the requirement for site visits before the 2027 declaration deadline, manufacturers are operating under a tight timeline. Importers that secure verified actual data from low-emission producers pay for fewer certificates than importers relying on default baselines.

The bottom line

Embedded emissions under CBAM are the output of a strict, product-specific accounting standard, not a general measure of a supply chain's carbon footprint. Because the definitive period is now in force, lacking granular, third-party verified data from the factory floor means exposure to inflated default emission values and the markups applied to them. Managing CBAM liability rests on precise data acquisition and site-level verification.
