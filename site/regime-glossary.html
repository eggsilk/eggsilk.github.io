CBAM regime · Understanding the regime

The CBAM glossary

CBAM terminology describes an ongoing carbon market risk rather than a fixed border tariff. This glossary defines the core terms an EU importer meets in the definitive phase, from the authorised declarant that carries the liability to the verified, precursor-level data that controls its cost.

In brief

  • CBAM terminology outlines a dynamic financial market risk rather than a static, one-time border tariff.
  • The January 2026 transition shifted the compliance burden from reporting estimates to purchasing certificates linked to volatile carbon prices.
  • Default values now carry published markups; verified, multi-tier supply chain data is the primary mechanism for financial control.

The financial shift and the authorised declarant

On 1 January 2026, the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism moved from a transitional, reporting-only exercise to a definitive financial obligation. EU importers of steel, aluminium, cement, fertiliser, hydrogen, and electricity no longer merely track emissions; they carry a legal requirement to purchase and surrender CBAM certificates.

This financial liability rests entirely on the "authorised declarant." The declarant is the legal entity, either the importer or an indirect customs representative, registered with national authorities and holding full legal and financial responsibility for CBAM compliance. For importers of any CBAM goods, applications filed by 31 March 2026 allowed continued importing while approval was pending; applications remain possible after that date, but importing requires granted status. Companies operating as declarants now face penalties for inaccurate declarations, non-compliance, or late surrender of certificates.

The financial mechanism the declarant manages is centred on the CBAM certificate. One certificate represents one tonne of CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e) embedded emissions. Unlike a traditional customs duty, the price of a certificate is not fixed. It tracks a quarterly average of EU ETS auction prices in 2026, moving to a weekly average from 2027. Because the EU ETS is a traded carbon market, the price of CBAM certificates fluctuates. The mechanism is therefore an ongoing financial exposure that requires budgeting and forecasting. Importers face a volatile commodity price rather than a static tax.

While the financial liability began accruing at the start of 2026, the first administrative payment cycle is still ahead. The actual sale of CBAM certificates to cover 2026 imports will commence on 1 February 2027, with the first annual declaration and corresponding certificate surrender due by 30 September 2027.

Calculating liability through precursors

The number of certificates a declarant must purchase is dictated by the volume of "embedded emissions" in the imported goods. Embedded emissions encompass the total greenhouse gases released during the production of the goods. This calculation includes direct emissions (Scope 1) from the production process itself, and for certain goods, indirect emissions (Scope 2) generated by the electricity consumed during manufacturing.

The assessment of embedded emissions runs over defined system boundaries, narrower than a life-cycle assessment. It is not limited to the output of the direct supplier's factory, because the regulation requires the mandatory inclusion of emissions from specific input materials, classified as "precursors". For steel goods, the calculation covers the direct emissions of the production route plus its listed precursors: sintered ore, pig iron, direct reduced iron, ferro-alloys, and crude steel. Upstream mining emissions, such as iron ore extraction, sit outside the boundary. Electricity used in production is reported as indirect emissions, and for iron and steel these carry no certificate obligation in the definitive period.

This multi-tier supply chain requirement presents a data acquisition problem. Many non-EU manufacturers, particularly those outside of highly regulated carbon markets, lack the systems required to track direct and indirect emissions, let alone the capacity to acquire that identical data from their own upstream suppliers.

To bridge this gap, EU importers have shifted from treating CBAM as a compliance checklist to treating it as a supply chain management problem. Importers frequently issue training materials and standardised questionnaires based on the European Commission's Communication Handbook to their suppliers. Because manual spreadsheet tracking breaks down across complex supply networks, companies increasingly deploy specialised carbon accounting software from providers like Assent, Sphera, Normative, and Coolset. These platforms automate supplier engagement, aggregate multi-tier data, and execute calculations aligned strictly with the EU methodology.

The cost penalty of default values

When an importer cannot acquire actual emissions data from a non-EU manufacturer, they must rely on "default values." Default values are pre-determined emission factors set by the European Commission. During the transitional period that ended in December 2025, the use of default values was permitted as a leniency measure while supply chains adapted to the reporting requirements.

In the definitive phase, default values function as a financial penalty. They are intentionally conservative, based on the average emission intensity of the exporting country for a given good. Relying on default values inflates CBAM certificate costs compared to using actual, optimised emissions data.

The definitive phase also introduced markups on default values to disincentivise their use. Applying default values incurs an automatic markup on the required certificates: for aluminium, cement and iron and steel, 10% in 2026 rising to 30% by 2028, and 1% for fertilisers.

Because default values guarantee an inflated financial liability, acquiring actual data operates as the primary cost-mitigation lever. Importers with verifiable supply chain data map their exact carbon footprint and pay only for the emissions physically produced.

A secondary mechanism to reduce certificate liability involves proving that a carbon price was already paid in the good's country of origin. If a non-EU manufacturer operates under a local carbon tax or trading scheme, the authorised declarant can claim a corresponding deduction on the number of CBAM certificates required. This requires auditable documentation detailing the local pricing scheme, the exact amount paid, and its direct correlation to the specific embedded emissions of the imported shipment.

The 2026 verification mandate

The financial advantage of using actual emissions over default values is contingent on a new hurdle introduced in the definitive phase: mandatory third-party verification.

In the definitive period, actual emissions data can be used only if it has been verified by an accredited CBAM verifier. The first declarations fall due on 30 September 2027, and the verifier registry and accreditation pool are being established through 2026. The requirement applies to the entire data package within the system boundaries. If a non-EU manufacturer provides data on a precursor, the direct emissions, indirect emissions, and production route allocations for that precursor must pass the same verification audit as the final product.

This shifts the dynamic between EU importers and non-EU manufacturers. Unverified actual data is effectively void; the importer falls back to the marked-up default values. Non-EU producers that can deliver verified data lower the certificate costs of their European buyers.

Conclusion

CBAM terminology outlines a shift from customs administration to ongoing carbon market exposure. Because the definitive phase penalises estimated data with escalating markups, the legal liability of the authorised declarant now hinges entirely on the ability to extract verified, precursor-level emissions data from deep within non-EU supply chains. Securing this data is the only mechanism available to control certificate costs and mitigate exposure to a volatile carbon market.
